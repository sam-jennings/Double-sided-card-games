# Collection Overview

*9 symbols · 36 cards · one deck · a collection of games at every stage, from first idea to proven at the table*

The master deck is 36 double-faced cards: every pair of symbols from a set of nine appears exactly once. Any game that runs on fewer than 9 symbols uses a stated subset — no separate deck needed.

A printable prototype deck (Set D Glyphs skin, duplex A4, cut guides) lives in [Print and Play/FLIP_DECK_print_and_play.pdf](Print%20and%20Play/FLIP_DECK_print_and_play.pdf).

For detailed, combinable filters in Obsidian, open [Game Catalog](GAME_CATALOG.md). For the public, mobile-friendly picker, open [games.html](games.html) (or start at [index.html](index.html)).

Two fields describe every game.

**State — what to do with it next:**

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **In development** | No usable rules yet, or rules that still need design work before they are worth tabling. |
| **Ready to test** | You could physically play it now without further design work. |
| **Proven** | Played, works, and has no known blocking problem. |
| **Shelved** | Deliberately inactive and retained as a design record. |

**Physically played — true once humans have played it on a table.** This never reverts. A game can be physically played and still *In development*, as JANUS is: tested, and found to need rework. Detailed verdicts live in each game's playtest reports, and `next_step` says what specifically needs doing.

## Development Status

Every game carries **one state** — *In development*, *Ready to test*, *Proven*, or *Shelved* (defined in [product-vision.md](.kiro/steering/product-vision.md)) — and a separate **physically played** flag that records whether humans have ever played it on a table. The state answers "what do I do with this next"; the flag answers "have I actually played it", and never reverts. Simulation, solving, and versions are recorded as `badges`, not state. Older Core Set and publisher-facing decisions have been separated into [Later/Publishing](Later/Publishing/README.md) and do not control current priorities.

### Proven — played, works, no known blocker

**BLIGHT** [Blight/](Blight) · 3–4–6 · ~25 min · Trick-taking (avoidance) · **Proven** · `physically played` · `sim-validated · first table test June 2026`

Avoidance trick-taker. Same engine, inverted: a captured card's penalty is its **hidden** pip — the back of the card is the poison. Lowest total wins; the rot pile is stored penalty-face-up so nothing is remembered. Shoot the rot (take every trick) to score 0. The dark sibling of GLEAN. Steepest trick-taking skill gap (2.02×/1.91×/1.41×); seat-fair.

*First table test (June 2026, 3P, one round):* the session's **standout** — Lauren read the rules unaided and called them clear, and said it was the game she'd expected from this deck. Played clean, no rules disputes. Defining moment: Arran held ~6 Crowns, sloughed them away (dumping hidden 9s into others' piles) and **won** — the slough-as-weapon line working exactly as designed, though it surprised the table (a good depth signal + a teaching note). Two open issues: large-hand handling (12 cards, both faces, no sort, realign every trick — cross-game F1) and a **fun split** (Lauren disliked a "bad hand with no recovery"; Sam/Arran noted two-faced cards give more recovery than normal trick-takers). Verdict: **iterate** — one of the collection's strongest current games. See [BLIGHT_playtest_01.md](Blight/BLIGHT_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: at 4P (9) / 6P (6) does the hand-handling load drop to comfortable? Over a full match (one round per player), does the hand-luck complaint wash out? Does the shoot-the-rot threat ever light up the table?*

**DOWRY** [Dowry/](Dowry) · 2 (3–4 variant) · ~20 min · Cut-and-choose bluff & deduction · **Proven** · `physically played` · `v0.2, sim-certified floor`

"I cut, you choose" with a second layer: the cutter splits three cards into a lot of two and a lot of one **and** chooses which face of each shows, so the poor-looking lot may be the rich one. Twelve offers, roles swapping each turn; pair-uniqueness bounds what a dressed lot can hide, and the public spread of captured cards is a deduction registry. Floor sim (~4,750 games): terminates every time, seats near-fair (a ~56/44 second-cutter edge, mitigated by alternating match-play), skill gap ~3.6×, and a deceptive cutter beats an honest even-splitter ~83%.

*First physical test:* the 2-player base game worked extremely well—possibly the collection's strongest game so far—with no blocking rules or handling problem reported. Verdict: **Proven**. See [DOWRY_playtest_01.md](Dowry/DOWRY_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: does that result hold over another match? How much of the dressing game survives between players with different memory skill? The 3–4-player modes remain separate, untested variants.*

### Ready to test — playable now, no design work outstanding

#### Physically played; a specific fix or re-test is queued

**TWELVE TRIALS** [Twelve Trials/](Twelve%20Trials) · 1–2 (+3P co-op) · ~15 min · **Ready to test** · `physically played`

Open-information puzzle. The 36-card deck hides 840 different "almanacs" — ways of sorting all cards into 12 perfect symbol-trios. Find the best one; your score is how few flips it costs to make the deal match it. Exact solver average: 5.3 flips; unoptimised average: 9 flips. Tiers set accordingly.

*First table test (June 2026):* Solo and open-table 2P co-op. The puzzle is engaging and the almanac's season structure surfaced as a genuine discovery moment. Two handling bugs: (1) sideways-orientation flip-tracking doesn't survive constant card regrouping — cards move too much between staging groups for orientation to stay reliable; (2) the game sprawls across the table. The player stopped counting flips mid-session to focus on completion difficulty. Verdict: **iterate** — needs an alternative flip-tracking method and a workspace convention. See [TWELVE_TRIALS_playtest_01.md](Twelve%20Trials/TWELVE_TRIALS_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: does a thoughtful solve land near 7 flips (untested — flip-counting was abandoned)? What tracking method replaces sideways orientation? Does a "completion mode" (no scoring, just solve) have value as a learning variant?*

**THE ORRERY** [The Orrery/](The%20Orrery) · 1–3 · ~15–25 min · Solo/co-op sorting puzzle · **Ready to test** · `physically played`

Open-information orbit puzzle. Sort the full 36-card deck into four orbits of 9 cards, each showing all 9 symbols once on its visible faces and all 9 once on its hidden faces. Move cards freely; every flip costs 1 point. The sky debt (over-shown symbols vs. target count of 4) gives an exact lower bound per deal. Sibling to TWELVE TRIALS via a different decomposition (4 permutation orbits vs. 12 triangles).

*First table test (June 2026):* Tested the 7-symbol Apprentice version solo with a pre-balanced deal (sky debt 0, isolating the grouping puzzle). The standard puzzle was genuinely challenging and satisfying. Key finding: the word "orbit" misled the player into expecting a single continuous cycle (Clockmaker), when a valid standard solution can contain split cycles. The player solved standard Orrery but could not solve Clockmaker at the table. Open information handled perfectly. Verdict: **iterate** — fix terminology, keep Clockmaker as prestige variant only, then test with random deals. See [ORRERY_playtest_01.md](The%20Orrery/ORRERY_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: does the standard game remain satisfying across multiple deals? Does the 9-symbol version feel manageable? Does the full game (with sky debt) feel distinct from TWELVE TRIALS? Do split cycles feel valid once the rules explicitly allow them?*

**TRIGON** [Trigon/](Trigon) · 2–4 players · ~15–25 min · **Ready to test** · `physically played` · `~23k simulated games · first table test June 2026`

Grid capture. Place cards, trigger symbol abilities, chain reactions. Three matching symbols in a row/column/diagonal = capture. Star is wild. Extensively simulation-tuned; now has its first table test.

> *TRIGON was renamed from SYZYGY; the pre-rename rulebook is kept as [SYZYGY_rulebook_archived.md](Trigon/SYZYGY_rulebook_archived.md) inside the Trigon/ folder as a design record.*

*First table test (June 2026, 3P):* the engine never got a fair run — the session was bottlenecked by **handling/onboarding**, not mechanics. Two HIGH bugs: (1) the **grid is not physically defined**, so play slipped into unfixed-grid (Instinkt) habits, which breaks the position-based ability requirements; (2) **reference overhead** — every player checked both faces' requirements and abilities every turn off a shared laptop, making turns very slow. Comprehension hurdles around requirement-vs-ability and chaining order/limits (the rulebook answers both, but they're hard to find mid-turn). Verdict: **iterate** — fix the grid (proposed: first card = centre cell, no new components) and the reference problem before re-testing feel/balance. See [TRIGON_playtest_01.md](Trigon/TRIGON_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: does centre-first grid definition kill the unfixed-grid drift? With references sorted, does capture cadence and the "active player captures everything" swing read as drama? Does a second game lift play out of aimless placement, or is the real skill hidden behind a near-random obvious line? (Re-test at 2P.)*

**WILDFIRE** [Wildfire/](Wildfire) · 3–6 · ~10 min · Party · **Ready to test** · `physically played` · `v1.2 — rebuilt July 2026 · sim-tuned · first table test June 2026`

Match-and-turn shedding race. Sim closed all three v1.0 questions (chain 2 / refusal 2: zero stalls, flat seats, 1.5–1.8× skill edge, ~5-min races); Hot Well was cut and replaced by the Inferno chain-3 variant; Slow Match errata'd to refusal-cost 1.

*First table test (June 2026, 3P):* taught itself with **zero rule clarifications** — a clean gateway that got two non-gamers flipping confidently. But the **chaining-as-decision hypothesis was refuted** (everyone chained whenever possible; the sim's bridge-burning tension didn't surface with new players), and skill perception was low (acceptable for a gateway). Surfaced the deck-wide **hand-ordering** problem (12 cards, no natural sort — cross-game F1). Fixes adopted: the well-exhaustion rephrasing, and a sim-checked *Salvage* variant (draw-two-play-one). Verdict: **iterate**. See [WILDFIRE_playtest_01.md](Wildfire/WILDFIRE_playtest_01.md).

*Rebuilt as v1.2 (July 2026).* The "felt reactive" verdict was diagnosed rather than retuned: at one pile a card matched the target only 2/9 of the time, so players averaged **1.11 legal plays per turn** and **~70% of turns held no decision at all**. The fix is **two piles** (legal plays 2.12, do-nothing turns 34%→17%) plus **dropping chaining**, which is only possible because two piles switch off the refusal pump chains existed to vent — and which restores the pacing two piles alone would have halved. A **Mirror** rule (both piles showing one sign skips the next player) was added on design judgement *against* the simulated skill gap, as the game's only immediate, visible, aimed consequence. That disagreement prompted [SKILL_GAP_METHOD.md](Project/SKILL_GAP_METHOD.md), a collection-wide audit of how skill is measured. See [WILDFIRE_excitement_diagnosis.md](Wildfire/WILDFIRE_excitement_diagnosis.md).

*Outstanding questions (v1.2): does anyone choose a card **because of the target it leaves**, unprompted? Does Mirror read as influence or as an unfair skip? Is one card per turn enough, or is Inferno (chaining restored) the better default? Is announce-rule policing actually fun?*

**GLEAN** [Glean/](Glean) · 3–4–6 · ~20 min · Trick-taking (accumulation) · **Ready to test** · `physically played` · `sim-validated · first table test June 2026`

Gathering trick-taker. Follow by either face; the hidden face wins the trick. Each captured card counts toward **two** sign-majorities at once — win the most of a sign to claim its pip value (Crown 9 highest). The bright sibling of BLIGHT: same engine, opposite goal. Skill gap 1.74×/1.67×/1.49× (3/4/6P); deal-sensitive, so played as a match with rotating lead.

*First table test (June 2026, 3P, Plunder only):* the **trick engine works** — follow-by-either-face was learnable for experienced trick-takers once "letter = suit, number = rank" clicked. But **pip-sum Plunder scoring was refuted** (totals ~110, tedious; high pips dominate both winning and scoring), and the **majority game — the intended destination — was never reached**. Public harvests drew a "feels wrong" reaction. Fixes adopted: Plunder replaced by a **proto-majority** count (0–9, trivial arithmetic), a *Closed Harvest* memory variant documented, and explicit rank-structure teaching added (F4). Verdict: **iterate**. See [GLEAN_playtest_01.md](Glean/GLEAN_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: does majority scoring (the real game) play well? Do face-up-but-not-inspectable harvests feel better? Does 9-dominance flatten under majorities? Does explicit rank teaching change the experience?*

**CROSSROADS** [Crossroads/](Crossroads) · 2 · ~20 min · Abstract · **Ready to test** · `physically played` · `v1.1, sim-tested`

Route duel. Sim proved ko alone never ends the game (100% of unlimited-flip duels looped forever), so v1.1 adds **Signal Fires**: eight shared flips, paid by dimming (rotating) the eight city cards — every simulated game now ends in ~20 turns. Measured seat advantage favoured the *second* player, so the start became a pie rule: higher pip total chooses who goes first.

*First table test (June 2026):* Tested a simplified no-flip version at 7 and 9 symbols. The 7-symbol deck was too sparse; 9 symbols worked well. The core connection mechanic produces interesting decisions. Two bugs found: first-turn paralysis (no obvious anchor for the opening move) and an asymmetric end-of-game problem (double-pass rule feels unfair when players are ready to stop at different times). An organic physical layout convention emerged (card touches source city, shows destination face). Verdict: **iterate** — the flip economy (v1.1's Signal Fires) is untested and is the priority next step. See [CROSSROADS_playtest_01.md](Crossroads/CROSSROADS_playtest_01.md).

*Second table test (June 2026, off-spec):* an **off-spec** session (drifted to 3P / 9 cards; Signal Fires barely used) that again failed to test the flip economy, but surfaced a **CRITICAL pass-to-win line** — passing every turn (building nothing, keeping every card as a contract) won overwhelmingly because the network connected nearly every pair anyway, so the "keeping a road deletes it" tension didn't bite. Also re-confirmed the **end-trigger unfairness** (now twice-flagged) and **14-card hands overwhelming first-timers** (9 felt far better). Verdict: **iterate, on notice** — the pass-to-win finding must be reproduced (or refuted) under **proper 2P + Signal Fires** play, where flips can deny a passer's routes; the leading candidate fix is promoting the **Toll Roads** scoring (rewards roads you built) into the base game. See [CROSSROADS_playtest_02.md](Crossroads/CROSSROADS_playtest_02.md).

*Outstanding questions (priority): under 2P with Signal Fires actually used, does pass-to-win survive? Does an eighth-dim end trigger fix the end-of-game unfairness? Do the recorded flip questions (does eight flips ache? does dimming read at a glance?) finally get tested? Does a reduced-hand teaching ramp cure the 14-card cold start?*

**OUROBOROS** [Ouroboros/](Ouroboros) · 1 · ~10–15 min · Solo serpent-building puzzle · **Ready to test** · `physically played` · `v1.1, revived · sim-validated 1.74×`

Lay the all-pairs deck into one serpent until it bites its tail; forced mismatches are *scars* (your score), and exactly one scar is provably impossible. Earlier physical play exposed handling and early-failure problems. The revived v1.1 design fixes the buried open-symbol bug by keeping both endpoints face-up; a Monte-Carlo planning bot put the skill ceiling at ~1.74× (n=9) / ~1.84× (n=7) over random. See [OUROBOROS_playtest_01.md](Ouroboros/OUROBOROS_playtest_01.md) and [OUROBOROS_design_analysis.md](Ouroboros/OUROBOROS_design_analysis.md) §6.

*Next test: does the v1.1 face-up-endpoint layout genuinely remove all lifting? Is hand stewardship learnable at the table? Does shuffle variance now read as tension rather than luck-blame?*

**CAIRN** [Cairn/](Cairn) · 1 · ~10 min · Solo chain patience · **Ready to test** · `physically played` · `v0.1, sim-validated`

Compact solitaire built for a tray, not a tabletop. All state lives in three short cairns plus a held hand: extend a cairn by matching its top sign and exposing the partner sign. CAIRN has reached a physical table, although the original detailed notes were not recovered during the catalogue migration. See [CAIRN_playtest_01.md](Cairn/CAIRN_playtest_01.md).

*Next test: repeat the standard trail and record whether the chaining choices feel meaningful, whether getting blocked feels foreseeable, and whether the difficulty ladder lands.*

#### Never physically played — rules read and vetted, awaiting a table

**TURNCOAT** [Turncoat/](Turncoat) · 2–4 players · ~15–25 min · **Ready to test** · `~22k simulated games`

Allegiance grid. Every card serves two signs; the visible face declares loyalty today. Flip an opponent's agent to defect them. Bury your own before rivals turn them. Built on the same engine as TRIGON but feels entirely different. Extensively simulation-tuned but never tested at the table.

*First table questions: is one activation per turn exciting or exhausting over 18 turns? Does the bury-your-own loop teach itself? Does Mask feel like a fun strong pick or a must-pick? Allied Houses versus free-for-all at four?*

**FACE VALUE** [Face Value/](Face%20Value) · 2 · ~15–20 min · Bluffing duel · **Ready to test** · `pacing sim-checked`

Heads-up betting bluff. Both players commit a duel card — the face you *show* is your only claim about the strength you *hide*. Raise stakes from hand, call to showdown (higher hidden pip; pair-uniqueness makes ties impossible), or fold to keep your card unrevealed. Cold Read: name the exact hidden symbol for double-or-nothing — a gamble early, arithmetic late. Pacing sim-checked (~12 duels, skilled bot beats random 94%).

*Test focus: fold frequency; cold-read timing; whether acting last reads as the advantage it is.*

**ICARUS** [Icarus/](Icarus) · 2–4 · ~10–15 min · Push-your-luck · **Ready to test** · `v0.1, sim-certified floor`

Flip cards from your personal tower to build a row of distinct signs; a repeat sign and you **fall**, losing the row. Land to bank it and pay a **toll** (burn your next top card); a banked row of 4+ **soars** (laid sideways) for double. The backless deck shows half of every gamble — your tower's top face is always public — and the public registry (every pair once, 8 cards per sign) makes the bust odds exactly countable. Floor sim (~50k games): terminates, no stalls, skill gap **1.55–1.61×** (2/3/4P); the toll and the soar are both load-bearing (without them, graze-and-bank dominates), producing a bimodal "land at 2 or reach for 4" arc. The collection's first push-your-luck game and a ~10-minute filler. See [ICARUS_design_analysis.md](Icarus/ICARUS_design_analysis.md).

*First table questions: is the counting delicious or homework? Is the k=3 reach-for-the-soar moment as tense as the bimodal curve implies? Do waiting players read the Fallen field or switch off? Does the later-seat registry edge show up?*

**CONCORD** [Concord/](Concord) · 2 · ~25–35 min · Fair division / set building · **Ready to test** · `working prototype v0.1`

Repeated “I divide, you choose.” The Divider splits six bonds into two offers, turns one card, and the rival chooses first; both players try to ratify three-card sign triangles. The complete prototype rules and its falsification gates live together in [CONCORD_design_proposal.md](Concord/CONCORD_design_proposal.md).

*First table questions: can players scan a six-card division without graph-search paralysis? Does the compulsory flip improve the cut? Does ratification timing create tension or only friction?*

### In development — not worth tabling until design work is done

#### Physically played, but it didn't work as designed; needs a rethink

**JANUS** [Janus/](Janus) · 2–4 · ~20 min · Co-op · **In development** · `physically played` · `first table test June 2026 — core concealment untested`

Each player sees one face of their own cards; the table sees the other. Co-operate to raise symbol-triangle gates using half-seen hands. Hint grammar à la Hanabi, but the deduction is exact (every pair is unique, so a single hint can fully identify a card).

*First table test (June 2026, 3P Apprentice):* the table **abandoned the fan-asymmetry concealment** and played open-information, so JANUS-as-designed was **not actually tested**. The session's value was a **blocking handling discovery**: v1.1's "face-down draw stack" is **physically impossible** on a backless deck — the top card's face always shows, exactly the leak v1.1 claimed to fix. Players hacked it with a spare Crown as a deck cover. Terminology drift worth noting: the group reached for "locks / keys / clues" over gates/keystones/omens. Verdict: **iterate** — JANUS regresses to "needs a sound concealment model" (opaque deck cover, draw-bag, or a no-draw deal) before its real hypotheses can be tested. See [JANUS_playtest_01.md](Janus/JANUS_playtest_01.md) and [Deck-wide Playtest Findings](Project/Playtesting/DECK_PLAYTEST_FINDINGS.md) F7–F8.

*Outstanding questions: with a real opaque-draw method, does the concealed game run without face leaks? Can the table sustain the no-self-inspection rule for a whole game? Under genuine concealment, does locking a gate produce the deduction payoff?*

#### Rulebook exists, not yet vetted for play

> FORKED TONGUE and THE COUNCIL have full rulebooks and only need a read-through to confirm they're playable — a single vetting session bumps either to **Ready to test**.

**FORKED TONGUE** [Forked Tongue/](Forked%20Tongue) · 3–6 · ~20 min · Bluffing · **In development**

Play a card and claim its hidden face aloud — truthfully or not. Challenge to force a reveal: liar takes the pot, honest player challenged means the challenger takes it. Because every pair exists once, the lie-space provably shrinks each turn.

*Test focus: ledger pacing; whether first-game players can price a lie.*

**THE COUNCIL** [The Council/](The%20Council) · 3–6 · ~25 min · Negotiation · **In development**

Three issues tabled each round; everyone secretly commits one card showing their chosen issue. Then, in seat order, anyone may flip their card to defect — to its one specific alternative. Lone backers take an issue's entire pot. Stacks grow for unresolved issues.

*Test focus: kingmaking at 5–6P; pot arithmetic staying frictionless.*

**SLEEPER** [Sleeper/](Sleeper) · 4–6 · ~25 min · Social deduction / hidden loyalty · **In development** · `v0.1, unsimmed by design`

The collection's first hidden-loyalty / social-deduction game. Each player guards **one** double-faced loyalty card — your cover sign shows, your secret **Master** sign is pressed to the table (the only persistent secret a backless deck holds cleanly). Steer the Court's decrees toward your master's sign, or turn a coat to swing one to its single printed alternative; when the deck runs out, everyone sworn to the most-shown **ruling sign** wins together. Termination is structural (one Court-deck card is spent per turn); unsimmed by design — its open questions are social, not arithmetic. See [SLEEPER_design_analysis.md](Sleeper/SLEEPER_design_analysis.md).

*First table questions: can a single flat loyalty card be guarded all game without leaks (the JANUS lesson)? Is the deduction real — do decree/flip patterns actually betray masters, or does the busy public row drown the signal? Kingmaking at the final reckoning? Does everyone serving the safest sign collapse into a shared blowout?*

**WISP** [Wisp/](Wisp) · 2–3 · ~20 min · Hidden movement / pursuit · **In development** · `v0.1, unsimmed by design`

The collection's first pursuit game — and the wildcard batch's first concept to reach a rulebook. One Wisp flees a side of one or two hunters across the complete graph, no board needed. Each trail card is laid **showing the place the Wisp left** and **pressing the place it reached**, so the chain rule confirms every position exactly one move late while the current place stays hidden. The hunters know the Wisp's whole hand by complement (24-card atlas → 12-card hand) and spend a budget of **4 Raids** naming a place; the Wisp escapes at an **8-card** trail, and two **sprints** bury an intermediate place the hunters can never Raid. The end-game excavation makes lying physically impossible. Drafted from the concept; **unsimmed and untested by design** (wildcard brief). See [WISP_rulebook.md](Wisp/WISP_rulebook.md) and §18.

*First table questions: does the hunters' candidate arithmetic read as a tightening noose or as homework? Is the base escape/Raid balance a chase or a coin-flip (or a move-3 slaughter)? And the one genuine handling risk — does an 8-card overlapped trail plus a squared sprint packet excavate cleanly without card slippage?*

**RELAY** [Relay/](Relay) · 2–4 · ~20–25 min · Network route-race / drafting · **In development** · `structural sim done — needs rulebook`

A clean-sheet networking game built to design out the four CROSSROADS table bugs. Every card is both a **directed road** (to build) and a **unique connection goal** (to claim); because every pair exists once, each goal is the only connection between its two signs, and a **flip re-aims** a road to make or break a route. Hand of three fed by an open draft (no 14-card wall); pass-to-win scores 0 by construction. Structural sim: terminates at 2–4P (0% caps), skill gap 1.9×/2.8×/3.6× (2/3/4P), flips drive ~22–28% of claims. See [RELAY_design_analysis.md](Relay/RELAY_design_analysis.md) and [NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md](NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md) §14. *(The edge case: simulation is evidence, but without a player-facing rulebook the game remains In development.)*

*Next step: a short deck-only rulebook, then a first table test. First questions: does the open draft cure first-turn paralysis? Do flips feel central or incidental at the table?*

**SNARE** [Snare/](Snare) · 2 · ~10–15 min · Perfect-information abstract / avoidance · **In development**

A silent two-player geometric trap: each placement colours an edge of the graph, and you lose if you are the one forced to complete a forbidden monochromatic triangle. It rides on the Ramsey fact that any balanced two-colouring of K₆ forces a mono-triangle — so on the six-symbol subset someone *must* eventually be snared, and the skill is steering the inevitable onto your opponent. Concept only. See [NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md](NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md) §10.

> *The wildcard batch (July 2026, §18–§22):* five concepts written to a deliberately different brief — maximum originality, rubric waived, unsimmed by design. Each turns a physical property of the backless deck into a genre. See [NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md](NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md). **WISP (§18) now has a rulebook but remains In development pending vetting.**

**STRATA** [Strata/](Strata) · 3–5 · ~20 min · Bluffing / deliberate memory / shedding · **In development**

Lies are never spoken — they're sedimented. Pressing a card onto a dig *is* the claim that the buried faces match; excavation is the audit; the both-faces orientation marker signs every card to its layer. Fills the flagged memory-game gap. §19.

**AUGURY** [Augury/](Augury) · 4–8 · ~30 min · Judged party / divination · **In development**

The Seer peeks a portent's hidden face and must speak one omen-sentence bridging shown sign to veiled sign without naming either; the table divines by pip-fingers, Dixit-curve scoring. The first game whose material is the Nine Signs identity itself. §20.

**EDICT** [Edict/](Edict) · 3–6 · ~25 min · Induction · **In development**

One player secretly enacts a Law from a printed menu of 18 and seals it in the deck's own alphabet (hidden pip + orientation); every play is an experiment, and the Line-and-Gutter layout keeps the whole lab notebook public and spatial. §21.

**VIGIL** [Vigil/](Vigil) · 2 · one move per passing, over days · Slow territory duel · **In development**

A game for a shelf, not a session: the 6×6 field *is* the save file — turn parity computes itself, claims lock by orientation, and scoring lines are shaped one irreversible flip at a time. A correspondence format the collection can reuse. §22.

> *Second flight (July 2026, §23–§27):* same brief, leaning into the first flight's two proven moves — physical inversions and format innovations.

**GOLEM** [Golem/](Golem) · 1–2 · ~20 min · Co-op boss fight vs. automa · **In development**

A backless deck can't hide its next card, so the automa doesn't try: the GOLEM's next blow is always public and the follow-through lands one beat too late — the telegraph rhythm of a video-game boss, on cardboard. §23.

**LACUNA** [Lacuna/](Lacuna) · 2–5 · ~15 min · Counting / deduction race · **In development**

The only thing this deck can perfectly hide is an absence: one card in an opaque sleeve, 35 both-face flashes, and a race to name the missing pair before a rival's confidence tells you they've finished counting first. §24.

**PADDOCK** [Paddock/](Paddock) · 3–6 · ~15 min · Betting race · **In development**

Nine runners with fuel tanks printed on the maths (8 cards per sign) but odds that stay honest intervals — and stakes hidden the only way this deck can hide anything: a pip pressed to the table under a public alibi. §25.

**HINGE** [Hinge/](Hinge) · 2 · ~5–10 min · Pocket abstract · **In development**

No session, no surface: the whole game is a 15-card packet held face-out in one hand — cut it anywhere and flip that block over as one unit, strike a run of three matching signs, play it standing in a queue. Renamed from QUEUE 25 July 2026 after the original rotate/flip mechanic failed solo playtest. §26.

**MIRROR** [Mirror/](Mirror) · 2 (or 2v2) · ~15 min · Co-op convergence / mind-meld · **In development**

Two provably isomorphic season hands, a prompt neither may show, and one goal: show the same sign your partner shows. Pure association early; by round seven you can compute each other's options exactly — empathy annealing into logic. §27.

**COVENANT** [Covenant/](Covenant) · 1–4 · ~15 min/chapter · Solo campaign / deduction · **In development**

A growing-deck solo campaign: you are the Tenth, earning each sign's trust one chapter at a time, and the sub-deck you have won *is* the save file (6 → 36 cards). Version A (The Loyalty Duel) fights each new sign as a blind-matching permutation trial — a row of visually identical attackers, each hiding a different won sign to be claimed from hand; matching flips stranger to friend and shuffles the card into your deck. §28.

#### On reviving a shelved game

*Shelving is not permanent. OUROBOROS — once the collection's only abandoned game — was **revived to the pipeline (now Ready to test)** in June 2026 after its reasons for being dropped were re-examined; see its entry under Ready to test above and [OUROBOROS_design_analysis.md](Ouroboros/OUROBOROS_design_analysis.md) §6.*

The OUROBOROS episode is retained here as the worked example of confronting-then-revisiting that decision. The lessons it taught remain binding, in their corrected form:

1. Solo games should avoid *involuntary* hidden-face bookkeeping — but **deliberate, verifiable memory or deduction is welcome when it is the point** (OUROBOROS's buried open symbol was forced overhead, now fixed by showing the open symbol face-up).
2. Any constantly-needed, low-payoff state must be *visible*, never buried on a down-face (orientation-as-state resolves it).
3. A thin skill gap warns of a dull game — but **read the gap honestly**: distinguish the *obvious* strategy's gap from the *ceiling*, and remember a one-ply bot is a floor (OUROBOROS's "thin gap" was a one-ply artefact; a planner clears the healthy bar).

### The component policy this reveals

- **State is encoded in the cards by default** — boards built from the deck (CROSSROADS), **orientation** (sideways = flipped, "orientation as state"), **position** (your pile/area), and printed **pips**. This is the first preference and what most games do.
- A **small, generic, reusable shared counter kit is sanctioned**, not banned. JANUS (6 omens) and THE COUNCIL (1 speaker token) already establish a house kit of "any small objects." A new game **may** draw on the *same* generic counters when they genuinely improve play — if a counter already earns its place for JANUS and helps elsewhere, that is a point in its favour, not a violation.
- **The graduated guideline for new designs:**
  1. *Prefer* encoding state in the cards (positional ownership, orientation, pips) when it's clean.
  2. *Acceptable freely:* the existing generic shared-counter kit (identical small tokens, player-supplied).
  3. *Justify at collection level:* introducing a **new class** of shared component (e.g., a set of small **colour-coded cubes** for per-player ownership). Permissible if it is small, generic, off-the-shelf, and **reused by more than one game** — not bespoke to one. The bar is "would several games want this kit?", not "never."
  4. *Avoid:* bespoke/printed/game-specific pieces, large components, or anything only one game could ever use.
- So per-player ownership markers are **not forbidden** — they need a shared, reusable, generic kit to justify them, ideally adopted by multiple games rather than introduced for one.
