# Collection Overview

*9 symbols · 36 cards · one deck · a collection of games at every stage, from first idea to proven at the table*

The master deck is 36 double-faced cards: every pair of symbols from a set of nine appears exactly once. Any game that runs on fewer than 9 symbols uses a stated subset — no separate deck needed.

A printable prototype deck (Set D Glyphs skin, duplex A4, cut guides) lives in [Print and Play/FLIP_DECK_print_and_play.pdf](Print%20and%20Play/FLIP_DECK_print_and_play.pdf).

For detailed, combinable filters in Obsidian, open [Game Catalog](GAME_CATALOG.md). For the public, mobile-friendly picker, open [games.html](games.html) (or start at [index.html](index.html)).

## At a glance

**37 games:** 3 Proven · 10 Rough edges · 12 Untested · 11 In the workshop · 1 Shelved.

**13 have been physically played:** BLIGHT, SILK CUT, TWELVE TRIALS, THE ORRERY, TRIGON, WILDFIRE, GLEAN, CROSSROADS, JANUS, OUROBOROS, CAIRN, HINGE, DEADLOCK.

Two fields describe every game.

**State — what a player can expect:**

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Proven** | Played and works. Just play it. |
| **Rough edges** | Has been played; works in general but has known bugs or open questions. Feedback helps — a table may even find the fix. |
| **Untested** | Complete rules, never played. The first table should expect surprises. |
| **In the workshop** | Not playable yet: an idea only, or rules with a known problem to solve first. |
| **Shelved** | Set aside, kept as a design record. |

**Physically played — true once humans have played it on a table.** This never reverts, even if a game later goes back to *In the workshop*. A *Rough edges* game such as JANUS has been played and found to need work, but a table can still play it. Detailed verdicts live in each game's playtest reports, and `next_step` says what specifically needs doing.

## Development Status

Every game carries **one state** — *Proven*, *Rough edges*, *Untested*, *In the workshop*, or *Shelved* (defined in [product-vision.md](.kiro/steering/product-vision.md)) — and a separate **physically played** flag that records whether humans have ever played it on a table. The state tells a player what to expect; the flag answers "has anyone actually played it", and never reverts. Simulation, solving, and versions are recorded as `badges`, not state. Older Core Set and publisher-facing decisions have been separated into [Later/Publishing](Later/Publishing/README.md) and do not control current priorities.

### Proven — played and works

**BLIGHT** [Blight/](Blight) · 3–4–6 · ~25 min · Trick-taking (avoidance) · **Proven** · `physically played` · `sim-validated · first table test June 2026`

Avoidance trick-taker. Same engine, inverted: a captured card's penalty is its **hidden** pip — the back of the card is the poison. Lowest total wins; the rot pile is stored penalty-face-up so nothing is remembered. Shoot the rot (take every trick) to score 0. The dark sibling of GLEAN. Steepest trick-taking skill gap (2.02×/1.91×/1.41×); seat-fair.

*First table test (June 2026, 3P, one round):* the session's **standout** — Lauren read the rules unaided and called them clear, and said it was the game she'd expected from this deck. Played clean, no rules disputes. Defining moment: Arran held ~6 Crowns, sloughed them away (dumping hidden 9s into others' piles) and **won** — the slough-as-weapon line working exactly as designed, though it surprised the table (a good depth signal + a teaching note). Two open issues: large-hand handling (12 cards, both faces, no sort, realign every trick — cross-game F1) and a **fun split** (Lauren disliked a "bad hand with no recovery"; Sam/Arran noted two-faced cards give more recovery than normal trick-takers). Verdict: **iterate** — one of the collection's strongest current games. See [BLIGHT_playtest_01.md](Blight/BLIGHT_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: at 4P (9) / 6P (6) does the hand-handling load drop to comfortable? Over a full match (one round per player), does the hand-luck complaint wash out? Does the shoot-the-rot threat ever light up the table?*

**SILK CUT** [Silk Cut/](Silk%20Cut) · 2 (3–4 variant) · ~20 min · Cut-and-choose bluff & deduction · **Proven** · `physically played` · `v0.2, sim-certified floor`

"I cut, you choose" with a second layer: the cutter splits three cards into a lot of two and a lot of one **and** chooses which face of each shows, so the poor-looking lot may be the rich one. Renamed from DOWRY on 19 November 2026; earlier records and the sibling naming brief use the old title. Twelve offers, roles swapping each turn; pair-uniqueness bounds what a dressed lot can hide, and the public spread of captured cards is a deduction registry. Floor sim (~4,750 games): terminates every time, seats near-fair (a ~56/44 second-cutter edge, mitigated by alternating match-play), skill gap ~3.6×, and a deceptive cutter beats an honest even-splitter ~83%.

*First physical test:* the 2-player base game worked extremely well—possibly the collection's strongest game so far—with no blocking rules or handling problem reported. Verdict: **Proven**. See [SILK_CUT_playtest_01.md](Silk%20Cut/SILK_CUT_playtest_01.md).

*Repeated-play update (recorded 7 September 2026):* Sam reports consistently great feedback across several playtests: everyone wanted to play again, and some said they would buy the deck for this game. Norma, described as hard to please with games, borrowed the deck to play with others and attempted a 3-player adaptation without reading the written variant. Its procedure and outcome are unknown. **Proven reaffirmed**; no core rules change indicated. See [SILK_CUT_playtest_02.md](Silk%20Cut/SILK_CUT_playtest_02.md) and [naming and sibling design brief](Silk%20Cut/SILK_CUT_future_directions.md).

*Next action: capture Norma's exact 3-player procedure and feedback before planning a multiplayer test. Open questions: what specific offer decisions drive the enjoyment, and how much of the dressing game survives between players with different memory skill? The written 3–4-player modes remain separate, untested variants.*

**DEADLOCK** (formerly VIGIL) [Deadlock/](Deadlock) · 2 · a two-game match, ~20 min (one game ~10) · Territory duel · **Proven** · `rulebook v1.0 · floor sim · played (Nov 2026)`

*Renamed and v1.0 (23 November 2026):* renamed from VIGIL, since the over-days "long watch" no longer describes it; a deadlock is the 3–3 line that scores for nobody. [DEADLOCK_rulebook.md](Deadlock/DEADLOCK_rulebook.md) v1.0 makes one sitting the main format and settles seat balance: the default is a **two-game match with seats swapped** (fair by construction, 45/53 in 500 simulated matches); for a single game, **South's point** (+1, first player wins about 38–47%) or **South's ties** (about 55–65%). The shelf format is now an untested variant.

*First playtest (23 November 2026):* played in one sitting with Noah, and very positive from both players: the right balance of strategy and simplicity. It took about 10 minutes and the strategy doesn't reward long multi-move planning, so it plays as a one-sitting duel and **does not meet the over-days brief** below. Kept as a proven game. Played with no seat rule, the first player won 13–11. See [DEADLOCK_playtest_01.md](Deadlock/DEADLOCK_playtest_01.md).

A game for a shelf, not a session: the 6×6 field *is* the save file — turn parity computes itself, claims lock by orientation, and scoring lines are shaped one irreversible flip at a time. A correspondence format the collection can reuse. §22.

*Rulebook draft and simulation (November 2026):* [DEADLOCK_rulebook.md](Deadlock/DEADLOCK_rulebook.md) v0.1 makes open cards sideways and claimed cards upright toward their owner, adds a North-vs-South count that detects a disturbed field, a one-sitting mode (~20–30 min), and repairs for knocked cards. Across 18,000 simulated games there are no stalls, careful play beats random **100%** (2.5× the points), and a player who may not flip loses 76%, so flipping is load-bearing. About half the lines are still live with ten cards left. But the **first mover wins about 70%** of careful games (78% with a two-ply bot). A provisional South +1 point gives 47/48 for the deeper bot but 40/57 for the shallower ones, with a third of games then going to the Crown tiebreak; won lines are worth only 2 or 3, so scores tie often. Needs a designer decision on seat balance. See [DEADLOCK_design_analysis.md](Deadlock/DEADLOCK_design_analysis.md).

### Rough edges — played, works in general, known bugs or open questions

Each of these shows a one-line **Known issue** on [games.html](games.html) and at the top of its rules.

**TWELVE TRIALS** [Twelve Trials/](Twelve%20Trials) · 1–2 (+3P co-op) · ~15 min · **Rough edges** · `physically played`

Open-information puzzle. The 36-card deck hides 840 different "almanacs" — ways of sorting all cards into 12 perfect symbol-trios. Find the best one; your score is how few flips it costs to make the deal match it. Exact solver average: 5.3 flips; unoptimised average: 9 flips. Tiers set accordingly.

*First table test (June 2026):* Solo and open-table 2P co-op. The puzzle is engaging and the almanac's season structure surfaced as a genuine discovery moment. Two handling bugs: (1) sideways-orientation flip-tracking doesn't survive constant card regrouping — cards move too much between staging groups for orientation to stay reliable; (2) the game sprawls across the table. The player stopped counting flips mid-session to focus on completion difficulty. Verdict: **iterate** — needs an alternative flip-tracking method and a workspace convention. See [TWELVE_TRIALS_playtest_01.md](Twelve%20Trials/TWELVE_TRIALS_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: does a thoughtful solve land near 7 flips (untested — flip-counting was abandoned)? What tracking method replaces sideways orientation? Does a "completion mode" (no scoring, just solve) have value as a learning variant?*

**THE ORRERY** [The Orrery/](The%20Orrery) · 1–3 · ~15–25 min · Solo/co-op sorting puzzle · **Rough edges** · `physically played`

Open-information orbit puzzle. Sort the full 36-card deck into four orbits of 9 cards, each showing all 9 symbols once on its visible faces and all 9 once on its hidden faces. Move cards freely; every flip costs 1 point. The sky debt (over-shown symbols vs. target count of 4) gives an exact lower bound per deal. Sibling to TWELVE TRIALS via a different decomposition (4 permutation orbits vs. 12 triangles).

*First table test (June 2026):* Tested the 7-symbol Apprentice version solo with a pre-balanced deal (sky debt 0, isolating the grouping puzzle). The standard puzzle was genuinely challenging and satisfying. Key finding: the word "orbit" misled the player into expecting a single continuous cycle (Clockmaker), when a valid standard solution can contain split cycles. The player solved standard Orrery but could not solve Clockmaker at the table. Open information handled perfectly. Verdict: **iterate** — fix terminology, keep Clockmaker as prestige variant only, then test with random deals. See [ORRERY_playtest_01.md](The%20Orrery/ORRERY_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: does the standard game remain satisfying across multiple deals? Does the 9-symbol version feel manageable? Does the full game (with sky debt) feel distinct from TWELVE TRIALS? Do split cycles feel valid once the rules explicitly allow them?*

**TRIGON** [Trigon/](Trigon) · 2–4 players · ~15–25 min · **Rough edges** · `physically played` · `~23k simulated games · first table test June 2026`

Grid capture. Place cards, trigger symbol abilities, chain reactions. Three matching symbols in a row/column/diagonal = capture. Star is wild. Extensively simulation-tuned; now has its first table test.

> *TRIGON was renamed from SYZYGY; the pre-rename rulebook is kept as [SYZYGY_rulebook_archived.md](Trigon/SYZYGY_rulebook_archived.md) inside the Trigon/ folder as a design record.*

*First table test (June 2026, 3P):* the engine never got a fair run — the session was bottlenecked by **handling/onboarding**, not mechanics. Two HIGH bugs: (1) the **grid is not physically defined**, so play slipped into unfixed-grid (Instinkt) habits, which breaks the position-based ability requirements; (2) **reference overhead** — every player checked both faces' requirements and abilities every turn off a shared laptop, making turns very slow. Comprehension hurdles around requirement-vs-ability and chaining order/limits (the rulebook answers both, but they're hard to find mid-turn). Verdict: **iterate** — fix the grid (proposed: first card = centre cell, no new components) and the reference problem before re-testing feel/balance. See [TRIGON_playtest_01.md](Trigon/TRIGON_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: does centre-first grid definition kill the unfixed-grid drift? With references sorted, does capture cadence and the "active player captures everything" swing read as drama? Does a second game lift play out of aimless placement, or is the real skill hidden behind a near-random obvious line? (Re-test at 2P.)*

**WILDFIRE** [Wildfire/](Wildfire) · 3–6 · ~10 min · Party · **Rough edges** · `physically played` · `v1.2 — rebuilt July 2026 · sim-tuned · first table test June 2026`

Match-and-turn shedding race. Sim closed all three v1.0 questions (chain 2 / refusal 2: zero stalls, flat seats, 1.5–1.8× skill edge, ~5-min races); Hot Well was cut and replaced by the Inferno chain-3 variant; Slow Match errata'd to refusal-cost 1.

*First table test (June 2026, 3P):* taught itself with **zero rule clarifications** — a clean gateway that got two non-gamers flipping confidently. But the **chaining-as-decision hypothesis was refuted** (everyone chained whenever possible; the sim's bridge-burning tension didn't surface with new players), and skill perception was low (acceptable for a gateway). Surfaced the deck-wide **hand-ordering** problem (12 cards, no natural sort — cross-game F1). Fixes adopted: the well-exhaustion rephrasing, and a sim-checked *Salvage* variant (draw-two-play-one). Verdict: **iterate**. See [WILDFIRE_playtest_01.md](Wildfire/WILDFIRE_playtest_01.md).

*Rebuilt as v1.2 (July 2026).* The "felt reactive" verdict was diagnosed rather than retuned: at one pile a card matched the target only 2/9 of the time, so players averaged **1.11 legal plays per turn** and **~70% of turns held no decision at all**. The fix is **two piles** (legal plays 2.12, do-nothing turns 34%→17%) plus **dropping chaining**, which is only possible because two piles switch off the refusal pump chains existed to vent — and which restores the pacing two piles alone would have halved. A **Mirror** rule (both piles showing one sign skips the next player) was added on design judgement *against* the simulated skill gap, as the game's only immediate, visible, aimed consequence. That disagreement prompted [SKILL_GAP_METHOD.md](Project/SKILL_GAP_METHOD.md), a collection-wide audit of how skill is measured. See [WILDFIRE_excitement_diagnosis.md](Wildfire/WILDFIRE_excitement_diagnosis.md).

*Outstanding questions (v1.2): does anyone choose a card **because of the target it leaves**, unprompted? Does Mirror read as influence or as an unfair skip? Is one card per turn enough, or is Inferno (chaining restored) the better default? Is announce-rule policing actually fun?*

**GLEAN** [Glean/](Glean) · 3–4–6 · ~20 min · Trick-taking (accumulation) · **Rough edges** · `physically played` · `sim-validated · first table test June 2026`

Gathering trick-taker. Follow by either face; the hidden face wins the trick. Each captured card counts toward **two** sign-majorities at once — win the most of a sign to claim its pip value (Crown 9 highest). The bright sibling of BLIGHT: same engine, opposite goal. Skill gap 1.74×/1.67×/1.49× (3/4/6P); deal-sensitive, so played as a match with rotating lead.

*First table test (June 2026, 3P, Plunder only):* the **trick engine works** — follow-by-either-face was learnable for experienced trick-takers once "letter = suit, number = rank" clicked. But **pip-sum Plunder scoring was refuted** (totals ~110, tedious; high pips dominate both winning and scoring), and the **majority game — the intended destination — was never reached**. Public harvests drew a "feels wrong" reaction. Fixes adopted: Plunder replaced by a **proto-majority** count (0–9, trivial arithmetic), a *Closed Harvest* memory variant documented, and explicit rank-structure teaching added (F4). Verdict: **iterate**. See [GLEAN_playtest_01.md](Glean/GLEAN_playtest_01.md).

*Outstanding questions: does majority scoring (the real game) play well? Do face-up-but-not-inspectable harvests feel better? Does 9-dominance flatten under majorities? Does explicit rank teaching change the experience?*

**CROSSROADS** [Crossroads/](Crossroads) · 2 · ~20 min · Abstract · **Rough edges** · `physically played` · `v1.2, sim-tested`

Route duel. Sim proved ko alone never ends the game (100% of unlimited-flip duels looped forever), so v1.1 added **Signal Fires**: eight shared flips, paid by dimming (rotating) the eight city cards — every simulated game now ends in ~20 turns. Measured seat advantage favoured the *second* player, so the start became a pie rule: higher pip total chooses who goes first.

*v1.2 (after playtests 01–02):* deal **9 roads each**, counted aloud, with the other **10 set aside unseen** — so the game now has a little hidden information: you know your own 9 roads but must read your rival's from what they build. **Toll** scoring is in the base game (+1 per road you built on a fulfilled contract's shortest route), which removes the pass-to-win line because a player who builds nothing collects no Toll. Roads are laid **touching their source city**, showing the destination face (the layout convention the first table found). Still open: the end-of-game trigger (the rulebook still ends on two consecutive passes; the candidate is the eighth city dimming) and official 2–4P support. See [CROSSROADS_rulebook.md](Crossroads/CROSSROADS_rulebook.md).

*First table test (June 2026):* Tested a simplified no-flip version at 7 and 9 symbols. The 7-symbol deck was too sparse; 9 symbols worked well. The core connection mechanic produces interesting decisions. Two bugs found: first-turn paralysis (no obvious anchor for the opening move) and an asymmetric end-of-game problem (double-pass rule feels unfair when players are ready to stop at different times). An organic physical layout convention emerged (card touches source city, shows destination face). Verdict: **iterate** — the flip economy (v1.1's Signal Fires) is untested and is the priority next step. See [CROSSROADS_playtest_01.md](Crossroads/CROSSROADS_playtest_01.md).

*Second table test (June 2026, off-spec):* an **off-spec** session (drifted to 3P / 9 cards; Signal Fires barely used) that again failed to test the flip economy, but surfaced a **CRITICAL pass-to-win line** — passing every turn (building nothing, keeping every card as a contract) won overwhelmingly because the network connected nearly every pair anyway, so the "keeping a road deletes it" tension didn't bite. Also re-confirmed the **end-trigger unfairness** (now twice-flagged) and **14-card hands overwhelming first-timers** (9 felt far better). Verdict: **iterate, on notice** — the pass-to-win finding must be reproduced (or refuted) under **proper 2P + Signal Fires** play, where flips can deny a passer's routes; the leading candidate fix is promoting the **Toll Roads** scoring (rewards roads you built) into the base game. See [CROSSROADS_playtest_02.md](Crossroads/CROSSROADS_playtest_02.md).

*Outstanding questions (priority): under 2P with Signal Fires actually used, does pass-to-win survive? Does an eighth-dim end trigger fix the end-of-game unfairness? Do the recorded flip questions (does eight flips ache? does dimming read at a glance?) finally get tested? Does a reduced-hand teaching ramp cure the 14-card cold start?*

**OUROBOROS** [Ouroboros/](Ouroboros) · 1 · ~10–15 min · Solo serpent-building puzzle · **Rough edges** · `physically played` · `v1.1, revived · sim-validated 1.74×`

Lay the all-pairs deck into one serpent until it bites its tail; forced mismatches are *scars* (your score), and exactly one scar is provably impossible. Earlier physical play exposed handling and early-failure problems. The revived v1.1 design fixes the buried open-symbol bug by keeping both endpoints face-up; a Monte-Carlo planning bot put the skill ceiling at ~1.74× (n=9) / ~1.84× (n=7) over random. See [OUROBOROS_playtest_01.md](Ouroboros/OUROBOROS_playtest_01.md) and [OUROBOROS_design_analysis.md](Ouroboros/OUROBOROS_design_analysis.md) §6.

*Next test: does the v1.1 face-up-endpoint layout genuinely remove all lifting? Is hand stewardship learnable at the table? Does shuffle variance now read as tension rather than luck-blame?*

**CAIRN** [Cairn/](Cairn) · 1 · ~10 min · Solo chain patience · **Rough edges** · `physically played` · `v0.1, sim-validated`

**JANUS** [Janus/](Janus) · 2–4 · ~20 min · Co-op · **Rough edges** · `physically played` · `first table test June 2026 — core concealment untested`

Each player sees one face of their own cards; the table sees the other. Co-operate to raise symbol-triangle gates using half-seen hands. Hint grammar à la Hanabi, but the deduction is exact (every pair is unique, so a single hint can fully identify a card).

*First table test (June 2026, 3P Apprentice):* the table **abandoned the fan-asymmetry concealment** and played open-information, so JANUS-as-designed was **not actually tested**. The session's value was a **blocking handling discovery**: v1.1's "face-down draw stack" is **physically impossible** on a backless deck — the top card's face always shows, exactly the leak v1.1 claimed to fix. Players hacked it with a spare Crown as a deck cover. Terminology drift worth noting: the group reached for "locks / keys / clues" over gates/keystones/omens. Verdict: **iterate** — JANUS regresses to "needs a sound concealment model" (opaque deck cover, draw-bag, or a no-draw deal) before its real hypotheses can be tested. See [JANUS_playtest_01.md](Janus/JANUS_playtest_01.md) and [Deck-wide Playtest Findings](Project/Playtesting/DECK_PLAYTEST_FINDINGS.md) F7–F8.

*Outstanding questions: with a real opaque-draw method, does the concealed game run without face leaks? Can the table sustain the no-self-inspection rule for a whole game? Under genuine concealment, does locking a gate produce the deduction payoff?*

**HINGE** [Hinge/](Hinge) · 2 · ~5–10 min · Pocket abstract · **Rough edges**

No session, no surface: the whole game is a 15-card packet held face-out in one hand — cut it anywhere and flip that block over as one unit, strike a run of three matching signs, play it standing in a queue. Renamed from QUEUE 25 July 2026 after the original rotate/flip mechanic failed solo playtest. §26.

*Rulebook draft and floor sim (November 2026):* [HINGE_rulebook.md](Hinge/HINGE_rulebook.md) v0.1 uses the concept's recommended strict alternation, a readable row, and a dry cap of 8 folds without a claim. But **careful players deadlock**: Skilled v Skilled claims only 1.6 of 15 cards, two-thirds of rounds are drawn, and every decided round goes to the first player (all claims fall on folds 1–3). Claim-and-go-again doesn't help. The two-player turn economy needs a designer decision. **FACE VALUE** [Face Value/](Face%20Value) · 2 · ~15–20 min · Bluffing duel · **Untested** · `pacing sim-checked`

**ICARUS** [Icarus/](Icarus) · 2–4 · ~10–15 min · Push-your-luck · **Untested** · `v0.1, sim-certified floor`

Floor sim (~50k games): terminates, no stalls, skill gap **1.55–1.61×** (2/3/4P); the toll and the soar are both load-bearing (without them, graze-and-bank dominates), producing a bimodal "land at 2 or reach for 4" arc. *First table questions: is the counting delicious or homework? Is the k=3 reach-for-the-soar moment as tense as the bimodal curve implies? Do waiting players read the Fallen field or switch off? Does the later-seat registry edge show up?*

**BRAVADO** [Bravado/](Bravado) · 3–6 · 15–25 min · Social push-your-luck / bluffing · **Untested** · `v0.1, exact odds + sim-certified floor`

JANUS's fan-asymmetry trick reduced to a **single presented card**, and the only other game in the collection to split one card between two audiences: a rival draws blind from the box and holds one card upright, so the active player sees one face and the whole table sees the other. You announce the sign you can see — which is what tells the Caller exactly which card they hold — and they answer with one word, *higher* or *lower*, knowing the truth and free to lie. Correct calls build a streak up to six; bank it or push and risk the lot. The Caller rotates after every card, so a long run reads several opponents rather than solving one. Also the first game to **teach rank as its central mechanic** (the pip *is* the odds table: P(higher) = (9−S)/8), which is the gap [GATEWAY_GAME_CONCEPTS.md](Project/GATEWAY_GAME_CONCEPTS.md) §13.5 flagged as the most actionable in the collection — WILDFIRE never reads a pip, and a player carried a sign-equals-rank misunderstanding through a whole GLEAN game because of it.

Exact enumeration confirms the odds table, the impossibility of ties, and the six-player supply bound (6 × 6 = exactly 36 cards). Floor sim (40k rounds per configuration): terminates at every count, skill gap **1.88×**, and a genuinely non-obvious optimum — **banking at three is the worst choice available** and pushing to five is the strongest single policy, because matching the leader loses the tie. Two honest caveats, both quantified rather than guessed: the Caller's word is adversarial cheap talk worth only **1.01–1.03×** at plausible read rates (so the game's headline mechanic lives or dies on human tells, with an *Accomplice* variant pre-designed as the repair), and the optional counting layer is worth just **+1.4 points of accuracy** — far thinner than ICARUS's. Seat spread is **10–13pp** at 4–6P and **19.7pp** at 3P, mitigated by the rotating start over a three-win match. See [BRAVADO_design_analysis.md](Bravado/BRAVADO_design_analysis.md).

*First table questions: does the room actually lean in when someone risks a strong streak — the one test that decides this design? Can anyone read a Caller well enough for the advice to beat simply reading the pip? Does the held card leak to the active player, or fail to reach the seats beside them? Does rotating the Caller create variety or cut the psychological duel short?*

**FAVOURS** [Favours/](Favours) · 5–9 · ~10 min · Party / simultaneous gift-giving · **Untested** · `v0.1, sim-checked`

The collection's **ambient party game**, and the only design that turns the deck's completeness into a *social* object rather than a mathematical one. Deal each player a sign and play with only the sub-deck those signs generate, and every card in the game names **exactly two people at the table**, with every pair of people appearing on exactly one card. Each round you secretly choose a card and give it to one of the two people printed on it; a favour arriving alone is kept, two arriving together overwhelm their recipient and all are refused. The sub-deck scales the game to the table rather than merely tolerating it — 5P/10 cards through 9P/the entire 36 — and the face pressed to the table names **the person you passed over**, who may turn it over and see. It is also the first game in the collection whose brief explicitly ranked the party criteria (one-minute teach, high count, survives an unrelated conversation) *above* the depth rubric.

Two bugs were found and repaired before the rulebook was written, both by simulation. In the original sketch **only the recipient scored**, which meant a player's own card choices contributed **0%** to their own score and crowding somebody else's gift was free — an omniscient spoiler denied exactly 1.00 opponent point at a cost of 0.00, making spite strictly dominant and a table of rational players score nothing. The fix is that **the giver takes a counter when their favour lands**, which prices crowding at exactly 1.00 for 1.00. Second, the spare cards at the even counts must be a **perfect matching** (Moon–Mask / Leaf–Eye / Wave–Flame at 6P; Moon–Star / Leaf–Key / Wave–Mask / Flame–Eye at 8P) — arbitrary removal leaves a sign-frequency spread of 2.06–2.31, the matching leaves **0**. Two honest caveats, both quantified: **43–55% of games tie** before the tiebreak (answered for now by the tiebreak and a two-deal variant at 5–6P), and the obvious heuristic — give to whoever has received least — **loses to random play at 0.76–0.84×**, which is the correct signature for an anti-coordination game but does make skill shallow. Design history and the five rejected sibling concepts are in [NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md](NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md) §31; simulation in [favours_sim.py](Favours/favours_sim.py).

*First table questions: does the table actually lobby — argue, warn people off, claim targets aloud — or does everyone silently compute the least-crowded recipient? If it plays in silence the social premise is wrong and this is an anti-coordination puzzle in party clothes. Do nine people track who is which sign from seating alone, or does the count-round-the-table lookup stall every turn? Do ties spoil the ending in practice, or does nobody mind?*

**CARAVAN** [Caravan/](Caravan) · 2–4 · ~15 min · Simultaneous cut-and-choose · **Untested** · `v0.1, sim-certified floor`

The first **sibling of SILK CUT**, and the collection's answer to a specific problem: SILK CUT is Proven and loved but two-player, its written 3–4P variant is untested, and any cutter-and-choosers version makes everyone but two people wait. CARAVAN runs every bargain at once. Each round the road points one way; you pack a dressed 2/1 offer for exactly one other player, exactly one other player packs for you, everybody takes at the same moment, and you keep what your taker leaves. The direction reverses between rounds, so **you cannot hit back at whoever is squeezing you — retaliation is a schedule, not an option.** The card flow is the design's neatest result: deal 6, refill to 6, and at 2, 3 *and* 4 players hands and stock empty together after exactly 12 offers and all 36 cards. Because every player reads a *different* offer, simultaneous choosing cannot collide, so it needs no written commitments and no components beyond the deck — the objection that shaped it away from the market design its brief recommended.

Floor-certified over ~4,200 games at 2/3/4P: **zero flow failures**, seats fair at every count (notably **no SILK CUT-style second-cutter edge** — 92/107 at 2P against SILK CUT's 56/93), take-decisions live 36–40% of the time against SILK CUT's 32% on the same instrumentation, and a thoughtful line beats a naive-but-sensible one by 1.22–1.29× on matched information with no bot reading a hidden hand. Two honest flags. **The road ablation is flat (0.90–1.01×):** a bot that ignores who receives the lot does as well as one that reasons about it, which is the design's whole premise; the rulebook carries *The trailing road* as the specific repair and question 1 of the playtest asks for it directly. Second, the simulation work turned up a correction to a Proven game — SILK CUT's published "dressing edge ≈1.6–1.7×" comes from an arm that changes the split objective *and* the dressing, and the matched dressing-only ablation is **1.11×** — plus a methodology finding that a smooth positional value proxy over-reports obviousness badly (74% vs 40% risk-free takes on the identical ruleset). Both are written up in [CARAVAN_design_analysis.md](Caravan/CARAVAN_design_analysis.md) §5.

*First table questions: does packing for one named neighbour actually change what you pack, or is the road decoration? Does simultaneous play feel social, or like three or four solitaires with no downtime? Is being unable to retaliate interesting or annoying? Is three rounds at 4P a game or a fragment?*

**LODGE** [Lodge/](Lodge) · 2–4 · ~15–25 min · Memory / swap-and-knock · **Untested** · `v0.2, vetted · floor sim at 2/3/4P (135k games)`

Beverbende (Beaver Gang) for the backless deck. Four cards in front of you; only the **pressed** faces score, and you start knowing just your two end cards. Draw from the stock (whose top face is always public) or the pond, keep a card by choosing which face to press, or discard it for a power: **Eye** peeks, **Mask** blind-swaps with a rival, **Star** turns any lodge card over so its *shown* face becomes the score. Knock to end the round; lowest total wins. The collection's most familiar entry into the flagged **deliberate-memory** gap, with no large hand to hold. See [LODGE_rulebook.md](Lodge/LODGE_rulebook.md).

*Floor sim at 2/3/4P (135,000 games, November 2026):* no stalls at any count; skilled rounds end by knock after ~2–2.5 turns each, so the stock never runs out; one skilled bot wins 96 / 91 / 87% of rounds against random (~1.9× on score); knocking early is not dominant; ~0.9–1.6 powers per round, so the power-card share doesn't swamp memory. First-seat edge is largest at 2P (58/42) and evened out by the rotating match. *v0.2 vetting:* three clarifications, no new rules: only one knock per round; discarding a stock card turned over makes it fully known; pressing the face the table saw on the stock is public.

*First table questions: is a ~2-turns-each round a sprint or too short? Is the Turn a memorable threat or a cruel swing? Does anyone use the shown faces and the pair registry to read a rival's lodge, or is it Beverbende with extra steps? Does the 2P first-seat edge show over a match? Plan: [LODGE_playtest_plan_01.md](Lodge/LODGE_playtest_plan_01.md).*

**COVENANT** [Covenant/](Covenant) · 1–4 · ~15 min/chapter · Solo campaign / deduction · **Untested** · `v0.1 rulebook, sim-validated (~145k hearings) · chapter one solved exactly`

A solo campaign where the deck grows as the story does. You are the Tenth, earning each sign's trust one chapter at a time, and the sub-deck you have won *is* the save file (6 → 36 cards). Version A, The Loyalty Duel: each chapter the newcomer's cards lie in a row of identical-looking strangers, each secretly bound to a different sign you have won. Claim one by naming its sign with a hand card and flipping it. A hit turns the stranger and your card comes back. A miss leaves it unmasked, and the stranger strikes back by burning cards. From chapter two your won signs give you powers. A grudge is the price of a Flame burn or of accepting a failed chapter. Assistants embody one sign each. See [COVENANT_rulebook.md](Covenant/COVENANT_rulebook.md).

*Sim (November 2026):* no stalls. A careful bot clears 90–97% of chapters two to six, while random play clears almost none. Ash per chapter climbs from 1.2 to 9.2. The edge over the obvious line is thin: 1.1–1.3× fewer cards lost from chapter three. Chapter one, solved exactly, is a two-minute teaching rite with one real decision (optimal 83% against 67% for careless play), and the rulebook says so. See [COVENANT_design_analysis.md](Covenant/COVENANT_design_analysis.md).

*First table questions: does the wash really hide the strangers? Does "risk the redundant card" feel like a decision or a chore? Is rising Ash enough tension when failure is rare? Do Wave, Moon, Key and Flame ever get used? Plan: [COVENANT_playtest_plan_01.md](Covenant/COVENANT_playtest_plan_01.md).*

**FORKED TONGUE** [Forked Tongue/](Forked%20Tongue) · 3–6 · ~20 min · Bluffing · **Untested** · `v1.2 · floor sim 24k games; end rules sim 2k/table, 0% stalls`

Play a card and claim its hidden face aloud — truthfully or not. Challenge to force a reveal: liar takes the pot, honest player challenged means the challenger takes it. Because every pair exists once, the lie-space provably shrinks each turn.

*Vetting and floor sim (November 2026, 24,000 games):* the rules read cleanly and skilled play beats random clearly (2.9–4.3× fair), but v1.1 games **failed to end**: tables of honest players stalled 36% (3P) and 79% (4P), because honest play never removes a card and a lap of plays then declines returns the table to where it started. **THE COUNCIL** [The Council/](The%20Council) · 3–6 · ~25 min · Negotiation · **Untested** · `v1.0a, vetted · floor sim 60k games`

**AFTERIMAGE** [Afterimage/](Afterimage) · 2, 3, 4 · 10–15 min · Memory / concentration · **Untested** · `v0.1 rulebook, sim-validated (~130k games)`

Concentration for a deck with no backs: all 36 cards lie flat, each showing a sign, and you hunt for two cards that *hide* the same sign. Flip one, then another; a match is yours and you go again, a miss stays flipped, so the sign it showed is now pressed underneath and lives only in memory. Every pair once and eight cards per sign make the hidden counts public, so late in the game memory turns into counting. The table never empties, so the game ends with a **Sweep**: flip everything, and if nothing matches you end the game and score 1. Ties go to the successful sweeper. A seven-sign, 21-card first-game variant halves the length. The purest form of the deliberate-memory gap; compare LODGE (private, partial memory). See [AFTERIMAGE_rulebook.md](Afterimage/AFTERIMAGE_rulebook.md).

*Sim (November 2026, ~130,000 games):* no stalls; perfect memory beats random every time; counting beats memory alone about 2:1; Sweep worth 1 decides 7–14% of results; the sweeper tiebreak leaves 0–6% shared wins with seats within 6 points. The memory gradient is steep (last-4 recall wins under 1% against perfect recall on the full deck, 10% on seven signs). See [AFTERIMAGE_design_analysis.md](Afterimage/AFTERIMAGE_design_analysis.md).

*First table questions (2P first; plan: [AFTERIMAGE_playtest_plan_01.md](Afterimage/AFTERIMAGE_playtest_plan_01.md)): can humans recall flipped cards well enough to beat guessing? Does counting feel like a satisfying "it's safe" moment or like homework? Does a strong memory win every time? Do sideways-marked flips help? Is a lucky first match noticeable?*

**SLEEPER** [Sleeper/](Sleeper) · 4–6 · ~25 min · Social deduction / hidden loyalty · **In the workshop** · `v0.1a · floor sim 18k games (Nov 2026)`

The collection's first hidden-loyalty / social-deduction game. **WISP** [Wisp/](Wisp) · 2–3 · ~20 min · Hidden movement / pursuit · **In the workshop** · `v0.1a · floor sim (Nov 2026)`

The collection's first pursuit game — and the wildcard batch's first concept to reach a rulebook. One Wisp flees a side of one or two hunters across the complete graph, no board needed. Each trail card is laid **showing the place the Wisp left** and **pressing the place it reached**, so the chain rule confirms every position exactly one move late while the current place stays hidden. The hunters know the Wisp's whole hand by complement (24-card atlas → 12-card hand) and spend a budget of **4 Raids** naming a place; the Wisp escapes at an **8-card** trail, and two **sprints** bury an intermediate place the hunters can never Raid. *First table questions: does the hunters' candidate arithmetic read as a tightening noose or as homework? Is the base escape/Raid balance a chase or a coin-flip (or a move-3 slaughter)? And the one genuine handling risk — does an 8-card overlapped trail plus a squared sprint packet excavate cleanly without card slippage?*

*Vetting and floor sim (November 2026; exact-deduction hunters, lookahead Wisp, 2,000 games per row plus a 54-setting sweep):* v0.1a fixes a hand leak (a held fan's outer face could name the Wisp's road, so roads are now chosen below the table edge). **The v0.1 dials are a move-3-to-5 slaughter:** careful hunters catch a careful Wisp 99.8% of the time. Balanced settings exist (~42–51% escapes, with skill mattering on both sides) but trade Raids against hand size. One Raid with a 12-card hand gives an early, one-shot chase; 2–3 Raids with a 14–16-card hand gives a real arc but a heavy hand. Needs a designer choice. **RELAY** [Relay/](Relay) · 2–4 · ~20–25 min · Network route-race / drafting · **In the workshop** · `structural sim done · rulebook draft v0.1`

A clean-sheet networking game built to design out the four CROSSROADS table bugs. Every card is both a **directed road** (to build) and a **unique connection goal** (to claim); because every pair exists once, each goal is the only connection between its two signs, and a **flip re-aims** a road to make or break a route. Hand of three fed by an open draft (no 14-card wall); pass-to-win scores 0 by construction. Structural sim (1,000 games per cell): terminates at 2–4P (0% caps), skill gap 1.9×/2.8×/3.7× (2/3/4P), flips drive ~23–27% of claims. *Rulebook draft (November 2026):* [RELAY_rulebook.md](Relay/RELAY_rulebook.md) v0.1 follows the simulated rules. Writing it exposed a handling gap: a road's source is its pressed face, so every road must touch a visible place marker. The removed Star cards (2P) and Crown cards (3P) supply the markers; **4P has no spare cards and is blocked until the designer picks a marker.** Three costed options with sim evidence are in the analysis: 2–3P only; 4P on 8 signs (sim 3.55× fair, deck-only); or nine external markers. **SNARE** [Snare/](Snare) · 2 · ~10–15 min · Perfect-information abstract / avoidance · **In the workshop** · `rulebook draft v0.1 · exact solve + floor sim (Nov 2026)`

A silent two-player geometric trap: each placement colours an edge of the graph, and you lose if you are the one forced to complete a forbidden monochromatic triangle. It rides on the Ramsey fact that any balanced two-colouring of K₆ forces a mono-triangle — so on the six-symbol subset someone *must* eventually be snared, and the skill is steering the inevitable onto your opponent. See [NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md](NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md) §10.

*Rulebook draft and exact solve (November 2026):* [SNARE_rulebook.md](Snare/SNARE_rulebook.md) v0.1 lays the houses on a diagonal **staircase**, so every snare is the same visible shape: three of your roads plus a house at the corners of a rectangle. Base game is the snare count (fewer wins); sudden death (*Sim*) and the five-sign Pentagon are variants. Across 87,200 simulated games there are no stalls, and careful play beats random **97.8%** with 3.4× fewer snares. But the single game is a **forced win for the second claimer**, careful play finds it with the strategy notes alone (100% of skilled games, 2–0 on snares), and the concept's pie rule is provably inert because every opening road is equivalent. The draft's seat-swapped match makes strong players draw every match. Setting one road aside gives about 50/50 seats but makes snares avoidable. Needs a designer decision. See [SNARE_design_analysis.md](Snare/SNARE_design_analysis.md).

#### Concept only, no rulebook yet

> Each concept has its own folder and game record. [NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md](NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md) preserves the broader design journal. Concepts appear in both catalogues with their state clearly shown, so they remain discoverable without being mistaken for playable games.

> *The wildcard batch (July 2026, §18–§22):* five concepts written to a deliberately different brief — maximum originality, rubric waived, unsimmed by design. Each turns a physical property of the backless deck into a genre. See [NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md](NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md). **WISP (§18) now has a rulebook but stays In the workshop until its balance dials are chosen.**

**STRATA** [Strata/](Strata) · 3–5 · ~20 min · Bluffing / deliberate memory / shedding · **In the workshop**

Lies are never spoken — they're sedimented. Pressing a card onto a dig *is* the claim that the buried faces match; excavation is the audit; the both-faces orientation marker signs every card to its layer. Fills the flagged memory-game gap. §19.

**AUGURY** [Augury/](Augury) · 4–8 · ~30 min · Judged party / divination · **In the workshop**

The Seer peeks a portent's hidden face and must speak one omen-sentence bridging shown sign to veiled sign without naming either; the table divines by pip-fingers, Dixit-curve scoring. The first game whose material is the Nine Signs identity itself. §20.

**EDICT** [Edict/](Edict) · 3–6 · ~25 min · Induction · **In the workshop**

One player secretly enacts a Law from a printed menu of 18 and seals it in the deck's own alphabet (hidden pip + orientation); every play is an experiment, and the Line-and-Gutter layout keeps the whole lab notebook public and spatial. §21.

> *Second flight (July 2026, §23–§27):* same brief, leaning into the first flight's two proven moves — physical inversions and format innovations.

**GOLEM** [Golem/](Golem) · 1–2 · ~20 min · Co-op boss fight vs. automa · **In the workshop**

A backless deck can't hide its next card, so the automa doesn't try: the GOLEM's next blow is always public and the follow-through lands one beat too late — the telegraph rhythm of a video-game boss, on cardboard. §23.

**LACUNA** [Lacuna/](Lacuna) · 2–5 · ~15 min · Counting / deduction race · **In the workshop**

The only thing this deck can perfectly hide is an absence: one card in an opaque sleeve, 35 both-face flashes, and a race to name the missing pair before a rival's confidence tells you they've finished counting first. §24.

**PADDOCK** [Paddock/](Paddock) · 3–6 · ~15 min · Betting race · **In the workshop**

Nine runners with fuel tanks printed on the maths (8 cards per sign) but odds that stay honest intervals — and stakes hidden the only way this deck can hide anything: a pip pressed to the table under a public alibi. §25.

**MIRROR** [Mirror/](Mirror) · 2 (or 2v2) · ~15 min · Co-op convergence / mind-meld · **In the workshop**

Two provably isomorphic season hands, a prompt neither may show, and one goal: show the same sign your partner shows. Pure association early; by round seven you can compute each other's options exactly — empathy annealing into logic. §27.

### Shelved — set aside, kept as a design record

**THE UNPLAYED PAIR** [The Unplayed Pair/](The%20Unplayed%20Pair) · 3–5 · ~25 min · Trick-taking · **Shelved**

One card is removed unseen before dealing; classic trick structure, follow by either face, with a final bet naming the missing card. **Parked (June 2026):** simulation showed the trick-taking and missing-card-deduction layers fight each other — certainty arrives only on the final trick, so the call mechanic is structurally dead (see [UNPLAYED_PAIR_design_analysis.md](The%20Unplayed%20Pair/UNPLAYED_PAIR_design_analysis.md)). The trick-taking slot is now filled by **GLEAN** and **BLIGHT**; the "guess the missing card" idea is preserved for a future non-trick-taking game (Parking Lot in [NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md](NEW_GAME_CONCEPTS.md)). #### On reviving a shelved game

*Shelving is not permanent. OUROBOROS — once the collection's only abandoned game — was **revived to the pipeline (now Rough edges)** in June 2026 after its reasons for being dropped were re-examined; see its entry under Rough edges above and [OUROBOROS_design_analysis.md](Ouroboros/OUROBOROS_design_analysis.md) §6.*

The OUROBOROS episode is retained here as the worked example of confronting-then-revisiting that decision. The lessons it taught remain binding, in their corrected form:

1. Solo games should avoid *involuntary* hidden-face bookkeeping — but **deliberate, verifiable memory or deduction is welcome when it is the point** (OUROBOROS's buried open symbol was forced overhead, now fixed by showing the open symbol face-up).
2. Any constantly-needed, low-payoff state must be *visible*, never buried on a down-face (orientation-as-state resolves it).
3. A thin skill gap warns of a dull game — but **read the gap honestly**: distinguish the *obvious* strategy's gap from the *ceiling*, and remember a one-ply bot is a floor (OUROBOROS's "thin gap" was a one-ply artefact; a planner clears the healthy bar).

### The component policy this reveals

- **State is encoded in the cards by default** — boards built from the deck (CROSSROADS), **orientation** (sideways = flipped, "orientation as state"), **position** (your pile/area), and printed **pips**. This is the first preference and what most games do.
- A **small, generic, reusable shared counter kit is sanctioned**, not banned. JANUS (6 omens) and THE COUNCIL (1 speaker token) already establish a house kit of "any small objects." A new game **may** draw on the *same* generic counters when they genuinely improve play — if a counter already earns its place for JANUS and helps elsewhere, that is a point in its favour, not a violation.
- **The graduated guideline for new designs:**
  1. *Prefer* encoding state in the cards (positional ownership, orientation, pips) when it's clean.
  2. *Acceptable freely:* the existing generic shared-counter kit (identical small tokens, player-supplied).
  3. *Justify at collection level:* introducing a **new class** of shared component (e.g., a set of small **colour-coded cubes** for per-player ownership). Permissible if it is small, generic, off-the-shelf, and **reused by more than one game** — not bespoke to one. The bar is "would several games want this kit?", not "never."
  4. *Avoid:* bespoke/printed/game-specific pieces, large components, or anything only one game could ever use.
- So per-player ownership markers are **not forbidden** — they need a shared, reusable, generic kit to justify them, ideally adopted by multiple games rather than introduced for one.
