# DOWRY — Playtest 02: repeated-play feedback

*Recorded 7 September 2026 · retrospective summary of several physical sessions, not one dated session*

## Source and setup

Sam reports having playtested Dowry a few times, with consistently great feedback. Exact session dates, number of games, scores, duration, opponents and rules version were not supplied. The report is understood in the context of the established 2-player base game; individual session player counts were not explicitly enumerated. No specific symbol subset or rules changes were reported.

## Evidence

- Sam reports that everyone has wanted to play again.
- Some players said they would want to buy the deck just to play this game. This is stated interest, not a purchase or a measured conversion rate.
- Norma, whom Sam describes as very hard to please with games, borrowed the deck to play with other people.
- Norma tried to make a 3-player version work. Sam has not tested that version himself, and Norma had not read the rulebook's 3–4-player variation. Her procedure and its outcome are unknown.

The borrowing is concrete evidence of interest beyond the original table. It does not establish that independent teaching succeeded. Norma's experiment is evidence of demand for multiplayer play, not validation of either her adaptation or the written variant.

## Bugs and rule changes

No specific bug or blocking problem was reported. No core rules change is warranted by this feedback. Preserve the successful base game while exploring multiplayer separately.

## Verdict and next action

**Proven, reaffirmed.** The repeated feedback strengthens confidence in the established game. It does not provide balance measurements or a controlled ranking against the collection.

**Next action:** capture Norma's exact 3-player procedure and feedback before designing a follow-up test around it. Ask how she dealt, formed lots, ordered choices, ended the game and scored; whether play completed; and what players wanted changed.

At the next base-game session, capture one memorable offer and why each player chose as they did. That is the smallest useful observation for distinguishing bluff-reading, majority tactics and deduction as the source of the appeal.
