# Beyond DOWRY: names, appeal and sibling directions

*7 September 2026 · design brief based on [repeated-play feedback](DOWRY_playtest_02.md) and the [current rules](DOWRY_rulebook.md)*

These are naming proposals and alternative design directions, not newly adopted catalogue games. The current title remains DOWRY pending selection. No sibling has been physically tested or simulated.

> **Update, 7 September 2026.** A fourth direction was designed and floor-simulated instead of Open Secret: **[CARAVAN](../Caravan/CARAVAN_rulebook.md)** — a fixed ring of simultaneous offers, now `Ready to test`. It reaches the same new question Open Secret was built for while removing that design's three stated risks (failed purchases, seller veto, written commitments); [CARAVAN_design_analysis.md](../Caravan/CARAVAN_design_analysis.md) §1 sets out the argument, and §5b records a correction to this game's own simulation claims found along the way. Open Secret, Sweetener and Terms of Exchange below remain unbuilt.

## A better name

### Paired names for DOWRY and CARAVAN

*Added 8 September 2026. Both pairs are options for consideration; neither has been selected or adopted. Current game titles remain DOWRY and CARAVAN. Name availability has not been checked.*

| Option | DOWRY becomes | CARAVAN becomes | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fair Share / Trade Winds** | **Fair Share** | **Trade Winds** | The clearest description of the games: a seemingly fair division with hidden possibilities, paired with bargains whose direction changes each round. Shared trading theme, with distinct identities. |
| **Velvet Cut / Velvet Road** | **Velvet Cut** | **Velvet Road** | A more visibly matched family: “velvet” suggests an attractive surface concealing a sharper bargain, while “cut” identifies division and “road” identifies the scheduled trading partners. More atmospheric, less immediately explanatory. |

Suggested straplines for the first pair:

- **Fair Share:** *You divide. They decide.*
- **Trade Winds:** *The wind chooses your customer. You choose what they see.*

The current recommendation is **Fair Share / Trade Winds** for clarity; **Velvet Cut / Velvet Road** is the alternative for a stronger shared naming identity.

### Earlier individual naming options

**Recommendation: VELVET CUT.** “Cut” names the actual action; “velvet” suggests a carefully dressed surface and concealed sharpness. It has a compact, slightly luxurious identity that suits the signs without requiring the marriage-property theme. Suggested strapline: *Two lots. Two faces. One difficult choice.*

| Candidate | What it promises | Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| **Velvet Cut** | Elegant division with a concealed edge | More evocative than immediately explanatory |
| **False Front** | The face you see may mislead you | Overemphasises deception; honest offers matter too |
| **Fair Offer** | A seemingly fair bargain with room for suspicion | Clear and teachable, but a fairly generic phrase |
| **The Better Half** | Choosing which share is really better | Memorable irony, though lots are deliberately unequal |
| **Silk & Stones** | Attractive packaging versus hidden substance | Most thematic; least explicit about the mechanism |

These are creative candidates; title availability has not been checked. A chosen name should receive one coherent live-document rename, retaining old playtest and simulation records as history.

## Why this one may be connecting

**Working explanation: players get to construct a dilemma, read another person, and then turn the result into a visible plan, with very little procedural overhead between those pleasures.** The feedback supports the appeal; it does not yet establish which mechanism causes it.

1. **You author the opponent's problem.** Choosing three cards, splitting 2/1 and selecting the shown faces lets you create a trap or a genuinely difficult bargain. The chooser has an equally legible act of agency: taking the lot they think you mispriced. It gives both people a reason to care during the same offer.
2. **Bluffing is embodied.** A Moon laid on the table can be a disguise for a Crown. Players can mislead through arrangement without having to invent a verbal lie or enjoy theatrical confrontation. A reveal gives the table a concrete moment to react to.
3. **Value belongs to the position, not simply the pip.** If you already have three Crowns and your opponent has none, another Crown may be less urgent than taking a Leaf lead. A card is also a choice between two scoring columns. The receiver helps determine whether the supposed gift was good.
4. **One card versus two is immediately comprehensible and rarely the whole answer.** Quantity supplies an intuitive starting point; hidden alternatives and majority thresholds overturn it. A player can enter through intuition before learning deeper counting.
5. **The deck's unusual construction serves the central decision.** One face advertises, the reverse offers another use, and unique pairs make some disguises impossible. The components explain the bluff rather than merely carrying it.
6. **Information changes the feel over time.** Captured pairs become inspectable, hands shrink and late cuts become more constrained. This is a rules-derived arc; whether players actually feel the growing certainty remains worth observing. Even at the end, opponents' private cards need not all be known.
7. **There is a clear route to a rematch.** A player can think “next time I'll read that offer differently” or “I overcommitted to Crown.” That is a plausible explanation for replay requests, not an observed player quotation.

The collection has uneven exposure, different audiences and different purposes. “Best-supported repeat-play enthusiasm so far” is more defensible than a universal quality ranking. The old bot results support the usefulness of deceptive cutting against the tested honest bot; they do not measure enjoyment or explain Norma's response.

## What a sibling should inherit

Keep small dressed offers, a consequential receiver choice, public ownership, two useful faces, inspectable revealed pairs and a short route from decision to reveal. Keep arithmetic and scoring visible. Change the central social decision, so that multiplayer is the reason for the new design.

The existing 3–4-player variant retains a rotating cutter and clockwise choice. It leaves dealing unspecified; 36 supports nine 4-card offers at 3P, but does not divide into 5-card offers at 4P. That is unfinished procedure, not a demonstrated failure at the table. A sibling should settle its own card flow explicitly.

## Three sibling directions

### 1. OPEN SECRET — rival stalls and selective bargains (recommended first)

**Pitch:** everyone dresses two lots; everyone shops at a rival's stall. A seller can make only one sale and keeps the other lot as their reward. An ignored stall earns nothing.

The new question is **“How do I attract the buyer I can afford to help?”** Buyers also read one another's intentions. Sellers need offers attractive enough to sell, while preserving the better remainder. That creates a reason for both honest advertising and concealment.

**First experiment, intended for 3 or 4 players:**

1. Use all 36 cards. Randomise faces and self-deal six cards each; public top-face glimpses are legitimate. Keep hands private, inspecting both faces freely. Remaining cards form an upright public-top stock. Everyone has an open scoring spread.
2. Everyone selects three hand cards and presents a dressed **2/1 split** at their own stall. Label the singleton lot 1 and the pair lot 2 by their size. Finish all offers before shopping; no inspecting reverses or changing offers after shopping begins.
3. Each player simultaneously commits to one lot at one other player's stall. For the first test, write seller seat number and lot size on a scrap of paper, cover it, then reveal together. This small extra component makes commitment reliable without pretending the cards have opaque backs. No self-purchases and no passing.
4. A stall with one buyer serves them. A stall with multiple buyers chooses exactly one of those buyers, who receives the lot they requested; the seller receives the other lot. Rejected buyers receive nothing from that attempted purchase. They still earn any remainder from a sale at their own stall. A player may buy once and sell once in the same round. Sellers with competing buyers commit their selection on paper and reveal together before any reverse faces are exposed.
5. If nobody chose a stall, all three of its cards go to an open discard history and score for nobody. **Do not let the seller keep unsold cards:** that would remove their reason to offer opponents anything attractive.
6. Reveal both faces of all offered cards, including unsold ones. Then, clockwise from a rotating first player, each person locks scoring faces for all cards they received and files them in their spread. Returning revealed cards to their locked orientation after inspection follows Dowry. Earlier placements inform later ones, so rotate who places first and record whether that advantage is noticeable.
7. Refill to six while stock remains, three cards per player in the same rotating order. Continue until all hands are empty. Rotate the first player each round.
8. Score Dowry-style: the unique leader in each shown sign scores its pip; a tied lead scores nobody. Most points wins, then most signs won, then a unique Moon-majority holder among tied players, otherwise shared victory. Discards remain inspectable but have no owner and never count for majorities.

| Players | Initial hands | Stock | Rounds | Refill rounds | Cards offered |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|---:|
| 3 | 6 each | 18 | 4 | After rounds 1 and 2 | 3 × 3 × 4 = 36 |
| 4 | 6 each | 12 | 3 | After round 1 | 4 × 3 × 3 = 36 |
| 2 adaptation | 6 each | 24 | 6 | After rounds 1–4 | 2 × 3 × 6 = 36 |

No partial refills or short offers are required. Every offered card leaves play that round, so termination is structural. This checks card flow, not balance or fun. Duration is unmeasured. Three rounds at four players may be too brief; first observe one deal, then test two deals with summed scores if players want a longer arc.

**Example:** Bea wants a Leaf to take its lead. Alex's pair advertises Leaf and Moon; its hidden faces include a Crown that Alex would prefer Bea not to receive. Alex must weigh a likely sale against that gift. Meanwhile Cora might choose Alex too. If both arrive, Alex chooses whom to serve and must honour that person's chosen lot. Bea can instead target a less contested seller. The split, disguise, destination and customer selection all matter.

**Why it is separate:** Dowry guarantees a bilateral division. Here several people simultaneously compete for counterparties, sales can fail, and a popular seller chooses whom to strengthen. Selling and shopping are distinct decisions made by every player each round.

**At two:** each player must shop at the other's stall, so both sales are guaranteed. This becomes simultaneous reciprocal cut-and-choose, with six-card hands and six rounds. It is a functional adaptation but loses the market's principal multiplayer tension; the original Dowry may remain the better duel. No dummy player is necessary.

**Risks to test:** failed purchases feeling like lost turns; seller veto becoming personal or kingmaking; everyone pursuing one leader-denial pattern; three rounds being too short; reading six or eight lots slowing play; and the paper commitment burden. Do not add money, powers or secret goals until the basic bargain earns them.

**Nearest collection neighbour:** FAVOURS also asks players to anticipate simultaneous destinations. Open Secret must earn its identity through dressed lots, seller choice and majority valuation; the crowd-reading ingredient alone is not new to the collection. Unlike FAVOURS' rejected collisions, competing customers here produce one selected sale.

### 2. SWEETENER — a public offer that grows until someone takes it

A rotating seller starts with one dressed card and adds another whenever all eligible buyers decline. The first acceptance closes the sale. Before opening, the seller sets aside a separate dressed commission card, which they bank only if someone buys; the receiver selects scoring faces for their purchase. A hard offer limit prevents indefinite passing, and an unsold offer plus commission is publicly discarded.

**Inheritance:** readable bait, uncertain reverses, public majority needs and seller-designed temptation. **New identity:** accept now or let someone else get the improving offer. Three and four players supply genuine competition for timing; at two it becomes a personal push-your-luck negotiation.

**Unresolved before a prototype:** exact deal and commission budget, response priority, whether a visible commission gives away too much, and whether players uninvolved in a sale wait too long. The rotating priority must offer equal opportunities. This is a promising direction, not complete rules.

### 3. TERMS OF EXCHANGE — trade a visible weakness for a hidden opportunity

Each player displays one dressed card for exchange. On a turn you propose a one-for-one swap using a card from your hand, showing one chosen face. The owner accepts or refuses. Accepted cards reveal both faces and go immediately into their new owners' locked scoring spreads; a refused proposal returns to its maker. A fixed number of proposal opportunities ends the round, with unsold displays publicly discarded, preventing an endless refusal loop.

**Inheritance:** purposeful face selection, double-use cards, evolving majority values and public evidence. **New identity:** consensual trading and choosing trading partners, rather than unilateral selection from a cut. At three or four players alternative partners make threats to walk away meaningful; at two that pressure is weaker.

**Unresolved before a prototype:** supply and round budget, whether refusal dominates, runaway cooperation between two players, and a clear boundary between allowed table talk and binding deals. This route has the richest negotiation potential and the largest departure from Dowry's effortless flow.

## First decision

Start with **Open Secret at three players**, then four if the first deal produces deliberate buyer/seller choices. It has explicit card flow and the clearest change of social structure. Log who buys and sells each round, failed purchases, reasons for selecting customers, and whether a concealed face changes a decision or reaction. Ask for an immediate replay before proposing revisions.

If players spend the session managing rejection rather than enjoying offers, try Sweetener next. If the strongest moments are people bargaining over whom they help, develop Terms of Exchange. Keep the proven base game's rules stable while these hypotheses are tested.
